## 1. What the regulators say about generative AI and confidentiality

### Takeaway
Every UK and US regulator that has spoken says the same thing in different words: existing confidentiality, competence and supervision duties apply unchanged; putting client information into a public or free AI tool is treated as disclosure to a third party (the SRA, the Bar Council, the BSB and a 2026 Upper Tribunal decision describe it as a waiver of privilege or a breach of confidence); and lawyers remain personally accountable for hallucinated output. Courts on both sides of the Atlantic are now imposing costs, fines and regulatory referrals for fake citations.

### Cited Findings

**UK: Solicitors Regulation Authority (SRA)**
- The SRA issued a warning notice on AI misuse on 17-18 August 2026 after "rising cases of hallucinated case law and confidentiality breaches". It says solicitors "remain accountable for their work and outputs, regardless of how that work has been prepared" — [Legal Cheek, 18 Aug 2026](https://www.legalcheek.com/2026/08/sra-warns-over-ai-misuse-following-rise-in-blunders/)
- The notice states that entering confidential information into open tools such as ChatGPT would amount to a waiver of privilege; "client information should only be entered into AI systems" with appropriate contractual, technical and organisational safeguards; it warns of "the permanent waiver of privilege that can occur as a result" and the "irreparable consequences" of sharing confidential client information — [Legal Cheek, 18 Aug 2026](https://www.legalcheek.com/2026/08/sra-warns-over-ai-misuse-following-rise-in-blunders/)
- Firms must have "effective governance structures, systems and controls in place to manage any risks" including from AI; Aileen Armstrong (SRA executive director, strategy and policy): "Firms and solicitors must have appropriate oversight and controls in place" and "Individuals remain responsible for the work they produce and the advice they provide" — [Legal Cheek, 18 Aug 2026](https://www.legalcheek.com/2026/08/sra-warns-over-ai-misuse-following-rise-in-blunders/)
- The SRA received 42 reports about potential AI misuse between July 2025 and July 2026 (figure attributed to Legal IT Insider, not the notice itself) — [Lets Data Science summary](https://letsdatascience.com/news/sra-warns-solicitors-over-ai-hallucinations-and-client-confi-2f083fd3); also reported in [Canadian Lawyer](https://www.canadianlawyermag.com/news/international/uk-solicitors-regulation-authority-investigates-potential-ai-misuse-by-solicitors/394529)
- Law-firm commentary on the notice says firms should check whether data is retained, used for training, where it is processed and what the contract says, and should approve tools centrally rather than leave individuals to decide — [JMW Solicitors](https://www.jmw.co.uk/blog/regulatory-and-disciplinary-proceedings/sra-ai-warning-notice-what-solicitors-and-law-firms-need-to-do-now)
- The notice does not create a new AI rule; it restates that existing confidentiality and supervision duties apply (I could not retrieve the notice text from sra.org.uk; all points above are from secondary coverage) — [Legal Cheek](https://www.legalcheek.com/2026/08/sra-warns-over-ai-misuse-following-rise-in-blunders/)
- SRA Risk Outlook report on AI in the legal market (November 2023): firms should supervise AI as they would a junior employee: "just as a solicitor should always appropriately supervise a more junior employee, they should be overseeing the use of AI" — [Local Government Lawyer summary](https://j5.localgovernmentlawyer.co.uk/55663-sra-issues-report-on-opportunities-and-risks-of-ai) (page could not be fetched; quote is from the search snippet)
- SRA Code of Conduct rule 6.3 requires solicitors to "keep the affairs of current and former client confidential" unless disclosure is "required or permitted by law or the client consents" — [Michelmores via LawCareers.Net](https://www.lawcareers.net/Explore/CommercialQuestion/Michelmores-LLP-Generative-AI-use-and-client-confidentiality)
- SRA Lawtech Insight (June 2025) cites Thomson Reuters data that the share of law firms using generative AI rose from 14% in 2024 to 26% in 2025 (cross-sector figure, not small-firm specific) — [SRA Lawtech Insight, June 2025](https://publications.sra.org.uk/lawtech-insight-june-2025/in-the-news)

**UK: Law Society of England and Wales**
- "Generative AI: the essentials" (published 7 August 2024), section 3.3: it is "generally advisable that you do not feed confidential information into generative AI tools", especially where you "lack direct control and oversight over the tool's development and deployment". For free online tools where you have no operational relationship with the vendor beyond use, you should "not put any confidential data into the tool" — [Michelmores via LawCareers.Net](https://www.lawcareers.net/Explore/CommercialQuestion/Michelmores-LLP-Generative-AI-use-and-client-confidentiality) (Law Society page returned 403; wording is as quoted by Michelmores)
- Michelmores' view: entering client information into a public generative AI tool is "arguably" a breach of confidence; "The act, by its very nature, is a breach of confidence"; public AI systems save all user inputs and may use them for training and to answer other users' queries — [LawCareers.Net](https://www.lawcareers.net/Explore/CommercialQuestion/Michelmores-LLP-Generative-AI-use-and-client-confidentiality)

**UK: Bar Council and Bar Standards Board (BSB)**
- Bar Council "Considerations when using ChatGPT and generative artificial intelligence software based on large language models": first issued 30 January 2024, reviewed 25 November 2025, author Samuel Townend KC. States there is "nothing inherently improper about using reliable AI tools for augmenting legal services" — [Bar Council PDF, Nov 2025](https://www.barcouncilethics.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/Considerations-when-using-ChatGPT-and-generative-artificial-intelligence-Nov-2025.pdf); [Inner Temple Library summary](https://www.innertemplelibrary.com/2024/01/new-guidance-on-generative-ai-for-the-bar-the-bar-council)
- Para 21: "ChatGPT and other LLMs may use the inputs from users' prompts to continue to develop and refine the system. In consequence, anything that a user types into the system may be used to train the software and might find itself repeated verbatim in future results. This is plainly problematic ... if it is confidential or subject to legal professional privilege." — [Bar Council PDF](https://www.barcouncilethics.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/Considerations-when-using-ChatGPT-and-generative-artificial-intelligence-Nov-2025.pdf)
- Para 28: "Be extremely vigilant about sharing with a generative LLM system any legally privileged or confidential information (including trade secrets), or any personal data, as the input information provided may be used to generate future outputs and could therefore be publicly shared with other users. Any such sharing of confidential information is likely to be a breach of Core Duty 6 and rule rC15.5 of the Code of Conduct, which could also result in disciplinary proceedings and/or legal liability." — [Bar Council PDF](https://www.barcouncilethics.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/Considerations-when-using-ChatGPT-and-generative-artificial-intelligence-Nov-2025.pdf)
- Para 29: "You should never input any personal data in response to prompts from the system." It notes the Italian Data Protection Authority fined ChatGPT in December 2024 over personal data, and that Italy, France and Spain have investigated OpenAI; UK GDPR applies — [Bar Council PDF](https://www.barcouncilethics.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/Considerations-when-using-ChatGPT-and-generative-artificial-intelligence-Nov-2025.pdf)
- Para 30: barristers should check the terms and conditions of any LLM to satisfy themselves it is compliant with Core Duty 6, rC15.5 and data protection law — [Bar Council PDF](https://www.barcouncilethics.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/Considerations-when-using-ChatGPT-and-generative-artificial-intelligence-Nov-2025.pdf)
- Para 11 cites the Stanford study "Hallucination-free? Assessing the reliability of leading AI legal research tools" (30 May 2024): Lexis+ AI and Thomson Reuters (Westlaw AI-Assisted Research and Ask Practical Law AI) "each relatively hallucinate between 17% and 33% of queries" — [Bar Council PDF](https://www.barcouncilethics.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/Considerations-when-using-ChatGPT-and-generative-artificial-intelligence-Nov-2025.pdf)
- Para 22: AI integration "introduces new attack vectors"; AI can be used for more sophisticated phishing and business email compromise — [Bar Council PDF](https://www.barcouncilethics.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/Considerations-when-using-ChatGPT-and-generative-artificial-intelligence-Nov-2025.pdf)
- Para 25: "Do not therefore take such systems' outputs on trust and certainly not at face value." Para 35 lists consequences: professional negligence claims, breach of confidence, data protection infringements, disciplinary action — [Bar Council PDF](https://www.barcouncilethics.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/Considerations-when-using-ChatGPT-and-generative-artificial-intelligence-Nov-2025.pdf)
- BSB guidance on AI came into force 18 May 2026. It says free generative AI tools are generally unsuitable for client work because their terms typically let providers assert rights over input data, keep prompts indefinitely and use them for model training; it also doubts such tools meet the conditions for outsourcing legal work. Court submissions, drafting and work with vulnerable clients are "high risk"; agentic AI needs "absolute caution" — [Legal Cheek, May 2026](https://www.legalcheek.com/2026/05/hands-off-chatgpt-bsb-puts-barristers-on-notice-over-free-ai-tools/amp/); [Law360 UK](https://www.law360.co.uk/articles/2477570); [Spencer West analysis](https://www.spencer-west.com/?p=7864)

**UK: courts and tribunals**
- Ayinde v London Borough of Haringey; Al-Haroun v Qatar National Bank [2025] EWHC 1383 (Admin), 6 June 2025, Divisional Court (Dame Victoria Sharp P and Johnson J). Para 6: freely available tools such as ChatGPT "are not capable of conducting reliable legal research" and "may cite sources that do not exist". Para 7-8: lawyers using AI for research must check accuracy against authoritative sources before relying on it; the duty applies equally when relying on work by trainees or AI. Para 87: the critical safeguard is checking AI output against an authoritative source — [Judgment, National Archives](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/admin/2025/1383)
- Ayinde: five cited cases did not exist (paras 36, 38). Al-Haroun: of 45 citations in a schedule, 18 cases did not exist and many real ones did not contain the quoted passages (paras 73-74). The solicitor had relied on his lay client's AI-assisted research without checking it — [Judgment](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/admin/2025/1383); [DAC Beachcroft](https://www.dacbeachcroft.com/en/what-we-think/ai-hallucinations-hit-the-high-court)
- Consequences: wasted costs order in Ayinde; placing fake citations before the court "will usually warrant a referral" to the regulator (para 29); both matters were referred (paras 70, 72, 81); contempt threshold found met in Ayinde but proceedings not started, expressly not a precedent (paras 68-69); available powers include admonishment, costs orders, strike-out, police referral and contempt (paras 23, 25) — [Judgment](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/admin/2025/1383)
- The judgment quotes the Bar Council: "Do not therefore take such systems' outputs on trust and certainly not at face value" (para 12) and a BSB blog: "AI, while a promising tool, is not a replacement for human responsibility and oversight" (para 14); para 83 gives examples from England and Wales, the US, Australia, New Zealand and Canada and says "many more examples could be given" — [Judgment](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/admin/2025/1383)
- An Upper Tribunal decision referred to as "Munir" (2026) is reported to have held that entering client data into free open AI tools "effectively puts it into the public domain" and is "an automatic breach of legal professional privilege", distinguishing open public tools from closed enterprise environments and placing responsibility for AI-assisted output on supervisors — [Legal Futures / Access Legal blog](https://www.legalfutures.co.uk/blog/containing-the-spread-of-unapproved-ai); [Resultsense commentary, 30 Jul 2026](https://www.resultsense.com/news/2026-07-30-shadow-ai-law-firms-munir-ruling/); Legal Cheek's BSB report refers to "a separate tribunal decision" that uploading client documents to ChatGPT places information "in the public domain" and waives LPP — [Legal Cheek](https://www.legalcheek.com/2026/05/hands-off-chatgpt-bsb-puts-barristers-on-notice-over-free-ai-tools/amp/). I could not locate the primary judgment; the commentary gives no citation or date.
- The judiciary's AI guidance is referenced in Ayinde para 16 as stating that legal analysis by current public chatbots does not produce convincing reasoning — [Judgment](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/admin/2025/1383)

**US: ABA Formal Opinion 512 (29 July 2024)**
- Lawyers must "evaluate the risks" before inputting client information into a GAI tool, including disclosure outside the firm and the risk "that the information will be disclosed to or accessed by others inside the firm"; the analysis is fact-driven (client, matter, task, tool) — [Zuva summary quoting the opinion](https://zuva.ai/blog/aba-formal-opinion-512/)

### Inferences
- UK regulators have moved from "guidance" (2024) to a formal SRA warning notice (August 2026), BSB guidance in force (May 2026) and a tribunal finding of automatic privilege waiver; the compliance burden on a small firm is now explicit rather than advisory.
- Both the SRA and the US opinions put the duty on the firm to read and understand vendor terms (training, retention, processing location). A sole practitioner has no in-house counsel or IT to do that review.
- The ABA's "informed consent, not boilerplate" standard means a small US firm would need a client conversation per matter before using a self-learning tool, which is impractical for high-volume, low-value work.

## 2. What exactly firms are afraid of

### Takeaway
The named fears are: prompts being used to train models and resurfacing for other users; permanent loss of privilege; vendor retention of prompts; personal-data processing outside UK GDPR control; hallucinated authorities that land the lawyer in front of a judge or regulator; and vendor terms they cannot assess. Surveys put accuracy first (75% in the ABA 2024 survey) and data privacy/security second (47%).

### Cited Findings
- Training and resurfacing: "anything that a user types into the system may be used to train the software and might find itself repeated verbatim in future results" — [Bar Council, Nov 2025, para 21](https://www.barcouncilethics.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/Considerations-when-using-ChatGPT-and-generative-artificial-intelligence-Nov-2025.pdf)
- Free tools' terms "typically allow providers to claim rights over input data, store prompts indefinitely, and use them for model training" — [BSB via Legal Cheek, May 2026](https://www.legalcheek.com/2026/05/hands-off-chatgpt-bsb-puts-barristers-on-notice-over-free-ai-tools/amp/)
- Privilege: "the permanent waiver of privilege that can occur" (SRA) — [Legal Cheek, Aug 2026](https://www.legalcheek.com/2026/08/sra-warns-over-ai-misuse-following-rise-in-blunders/); Heppner treated the AI provider as a third party — [WLF](https://www.wlf.org/2026/04/27/publishing/united-states-v-heppner-use-of-generative-ai-can-waive-privileges/)
- Before Heppner, commentary noted "no published case law addresses whether AI provider access waives privilege", and that some firms use separate tenants for privileged work — [Copilot Consulting](https://www.copilotconsulting.com/insights/microsoft-copilot-legal-document-review-contract-analysis)

- Copilot oversharing: it can "share confidential information with unauthorized firm personnel if data permissions aren't appropriately established" (Nelson Mullins, large firm) — [Intapp case study](https://www.intapp.com/case-study/nelson-mullins-copilot-ai-permissions/); the ABA example is a partner's compensation memo surfacing to anyone with permission to see it — [ABA Law Technology Today, 2026](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/law_practice/resources/law-technology-today/2026/what-should-law-firms-do-before-deploying-copilot/)
- Personal data: "You should never input any personal data in response to prompts from the system"; Italian DPA fined OpenAI December 2024 — [Bar Council para 29](https://www.barcouncilethics.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/Considerations-when-using-ChatGPT-and-generative-artificial-intelligence-Nov-2025.pdf)
- Hallucination rates even in paid legal tools: 17-33% of queries (Stanford, May 2024) — [Bar Council para 11](https://www.barcouncilethics.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/Considerations-when-using-ChatGPT-and-generative-artificial-intelligence-Nov-2025.pdf)
- ABA 2024 Legal Technology Survey (reported 7 March 2025; ABA members in private practice, average 28 years in practice): accuracy top concern 75%, reliability 56%, data privacy and security 47% — [LawNext, Apr 2025](https://www.lawnext.com/2025/04/an-ai-assisted-look-at-four-new-surveys-on-ai-adoption-in-law-how-do-they-compare-differ.html)
- AffiniPay 2025 Legal Industry Report (n>2,800, many solos and small firms): trustworthiness 42%, ethical issues 42%, technological maturity 41%, privilege concerns 36%; 60% unsure when their firm would adopt, citing training and policy gaps — [LawNext](https://www.lawnext.com/2025/04/an-ai-assisted-look-at-four-new-surveys-on-ai-adoption-in-law-how-do-they-compare-differ.html)
- Smokeball 2025 (US small firms/solos): ethical concerns 53%; Thomson Reuters 2025 (n=1,702, cross-profession): 52% had no AI policies, 64% had no gen AI training — [LawNext](https://www.lawnext.com/2025/04/an-ai-assisted-look-at-four-new-surveys-on-ai-adoption-in-law-how-do-they-compare-differ.html)
- Clio 2024 (secondary summary): 58% of legal professionals believe AI isn't yet advanced enough to be reliable; 39% don't trust it — [ALPMA blog](https://www.alpma.com.au/blog/the-critical-role-tech-adoption-plays-in-empowering-smaller-law-firms/) (not verified against Clio's report)
- Thomson Reuters 2023 (400+ respondents, US/UK/Canada): ~82% agreed GAI could be applied to legal work; only about half thought it should be, "amid serious concerns around accuracy and privacy" — [Global Legal Post](https://www.globallegalpost.com/news/one-in-five-large-law-firms-issue-warnings-over-use-of-generative-ai-or-chatgpt-survey-finds-420104522)
- Vendor terms firms cannot read: the SRA expects firms to understand "a provider's contractual and technical safeguards" and judge suitability — [Lets Data Science](https://letsdatascience.com/news/sra-warns-solicitors-over-ai-hallucinations-and-client-confi-2f083fd3); KBA: "review the provider's privacy policies and its disclaimers" — [KBA E-457](https://kybar.org/Portals/0/Admin/Ethics%20Opinions/KBA%20E-457.pdf)
- Audit trail: outside counsel guidelines from financial services, healthcare and government clients "increasingly require explicit pre-approval before client data is processed by any AI tool"; some "mandate audit trails", role-based access controls or disclosure of tools by name — [Intapp blog](https://www.intapp.com/blog/law-firm-ai-governance-ocg-compliance/)
- Some clients "are prohibiting the use of these tools" — [Law360](https://law360.com/articles/1891310/two-years-in-law-firms-see-real-results-from-generative-ai); a Kentucky Bar guide suggests clients ask outside counsel to confirm the AI tool "prohibits model training on your data" — [Louisville Bar Briefs, Dec 2025](https://www.loubar.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/Bar-Briefs_December25_Navigating-AI-with-Outside-Counsel_Crosby-and-Lavanga-1.pdf)
- Data leaving jurisdiction: Judge Vaden's standing order requires a certificate that GAI use "didn't disclose proprietary information to unauthorized parties" — [Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/esg/ai-standing-orders-proliferate-as-federal-courts-forge-own-paths); see section 6.

### Inferences
- The fear ranking is consistent across UK and US sources: hallucination (because it is visible, sanctioned and public) first; privilege/training second; vendor-term opacity third.
- For small firms, the privilege fear is now backed by at least one UK tribunal finding and one SDNY ruling, so "we might lose privilege" is no longer hypothetical.
- The 30-day Copilot retention claim is the kind of detail small firms repeat without checking; it illustrates the "vendor terms they cannot read" problem rather than a confirmed current term.

### Gaps
- No survey found that asks small UK firms specifically to rank confidentiality versus accuracy fears (UK figures are from Legal Futures/Access Legal and are vendor-run).
- Current Microsoft Copilot retention terms not verified from Microsoft documentation.

## 3. What firm AI policies look like in practice (bans, "no client data", approved lists, shadow AI)

### Takeaway
UK data: about 60% of fee-earners in small and mid-sized firms admit using unapproved tools (free ChatGPT), while 68% of firm leaders believe they have full visibility and zero risk. US: in-house teams report 81-83% unapproved use; 52% of professionals report no AI policy at all. Large firms issue formal warnings (21%); small firms more often have no policy.

### Cited Findings
- Access Legal survey (Censuswide, April 2026, n=200: 100 firm leaders, 100 practitioners; published May 2026): 59% ("nearly 60%") of UK fee-earners in small and mid-sized firms admit using unapproved tools such as free ChatGPT and Google AI search; 68% of firm leaders are "confident they have full visibility and zero risk of unapproved AI being used for client work" — [Legal Futures blog](https://www.legalfutures.co.uk/blog/containing-the-spread-of-unapproved-ai); [Solicitors Journal](https://www.solicitorsjournal.com/sjarticle/unapproved-ai-use-is-widespread-in-uk-law-firms--); vendor-run, self-reported — [Resultsense caveat](https://www.resultsense.com/news/2026-07-30-shadow-ai-law-firms-munir-ruling/)
- Same study: half of surveyed professionals would welcome AI built into their case management system but only 25% of firms have it; unapproved tools create "a patchwork of unaccounted-for applications"; "Restricting or banning AI may push staff toward shadow tools" — [Legal Futures blog](https://www.legalfutures.co.uk/blog/containing-the-spread-of-unapproved-ai)
- Axiom survey of 300 in-house counsel (companies over $50m revenue, not law firms): 83% of legal teams use AI tools not provided by their company; 81% use tools not formally approved; 47% have no policies — [Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/business-and-practice/sure-youve-got-an-ai-policy-but-is-everyone-following-it)

- A Fisher Phillips lawyer told Bloomberg the statistics "probably understate the degree to which lawyers are already using AI" — [Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/business-and-practice/sure-youve-got-an-ai-policy-but-is-everyone-following-it)
- Thomson Reuters 2023: ~15% of respondents said their firm had issued a warning about generative AI or ChatGPT at work, rising to 21% at large firms — [Global Legal Post](https://www.globallegalpost.com/news/one-in-five-large-law-firms-issue-warnings-over-use-of-generative-ai-or-chatgpt-survey-finds-420104522)
- Thomson Reuters 2025: 52% reported no AI policies; 64% had no gen AI training at work (cross-profession, n=1,702) — [LawNext](https://www.lawnext.com/2025/04/an-ai-assisted-look-at-four-new-surveys-on-ai-adoption-in-law-how-do-they-compare-differ.html)
- Adoption context: Smokeball 2025 State of Law (n=310, US): 53% of small firms and solos integrating gen AI, up from 27% in 2023 — [LawNext, Mar 2025](https://www.lawnext.com/2025/03/ai-adoption-nearly-doubles-among-small-law-firms-according-to-smokeballs-2025-state-of-law-report.html); Clio 2026: 71% of solos and 75% of small firms; ABA: firm AI use rose from 11% to 30% in 2024 — [NC Bar, May 2026](https://ncbar.org/nc-lawyer/2026-05/by-the-numbers-what-surveys-show-about-law-firm-ai-adoption)
- Firm policy expectations from the SRA notice: firms should "approve tools centrally rather than leave individuals to decide" and check retention, training, processing location and contract — [JMW](https://www.jmw.co.uk/blog/regulatory-and-disciplinary-proceedings/sra-ai-warning-notice-what-solicitors-and-law-firms-need-to-do-now); enterprise tools offer "much stronger confidentiality protection than ChatGPT, but staff still need training and clear usage policies" — [DWF](https://dwfgroup.com/fr-be/news-and-insights/insights/2026/8/prompt-review-repeat-a-guide-to-ai-for-trainees-and-junior-solicitors)
- Typical outside-counsel-guideline clauses: disclosure or written consent before AI use, approved tools only, keep client data out of public models, no billing for AI time savings, human review — [Layer3 Labs guide](https://www.layer3labs.io/guides/ai-provisions-outside-counsel-guidelines); "firms should not process client data through external AI systems without prior approval" — [Conventus Law](https://conventuslaw.com/report/how-to-write-outside-counsel-guidelines-that-govern-ai-assisted-work/)
- Copilot deployment concern: firms must get document permissions, email access rules and sensitivity labels right before enabling Copilot; labels, when configured, "block Copilot from including certain documents in its responses" — [ABA Law Technology Today, 2026](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/law_practice/resources/law-technology-today/2026/what-should-law-firms-do-before-deploying-copilot/); [CMIT](https://cmitsolutions.com/boise-id-1183/blog/how-law-firms-can-use-microsofts-ai-tools-without-compromising-client-confidentiality/)
- The Bar Council notes ChatGPT "shares technology with Microsoft Copilot" (footnote 2) — [Bar Council PDF](https://www.barcouncilethics.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/Considerations-when-using-ChatGPT-and-generative-artificial-intelligence-Nov-2025.pdf)

### Inferences
- The 60% vs 68% gap is the clearest single number on shadow AI in UK small firms: leaders think there is no problem, staff say otherwise.
- Small firms sit between two pressures: regulators (SRA, BSB) say free tools are unsuitable; staff use them anyway because approved alternatives are absent (only 25% have AI in their case management system).
- No source documents a representative count of UK small firms with an outright ChatGPT ban; the evidence is of warnings (large firms) and absence of policy (small firms).

### Gaps
- No independent (non-vendor) survey of UK small-firm AI policies found. Access Legal, DeepL, Axiom, Smokeball and Clio are all vendors.
- No figure found for the share of small firms with a written "no client data in AI" rule or an approved-tools list.
- No data found on Copilot adoption or concerns specifically in small firms (sources are large-firm case studies and consultants).

## 4. Cyber incidents hitting small firms

### Takeaway
UK: the SRA's 40-firm review found 75% had been targeted, 43% of attacks hit small firms, over £4m of client money was stolen and email modification fraud was about half of attacks. The ICO has fined or reprimanded three small criminal-law firms (Tuckers, Levales, DPP) for missing MFA, unpatched systems and unencrypted data. US: 27-29% of ABA-surveyed firms report a breach; only 19% of solos and 2-9 lawyer firms have an incident response plan. Ransom demands on the legal sector rose 60% to US$611,000 in 2025.

### Cited Findings

**UK sector-wide (NCSC Cyber Threat Report: UK Legal Sector, June 2023, prepared with the Law Society, Bar Council, SRA, Action Fraud and NCA)**
- Sector: over 32,900 enterprises, £43.9bn revenue, 320,000 people; over 230,000 solicitors and legal executives; 18,000 barristers (around 700 sole practices, 400 chambers) — [NCSC PDF](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/files/Cyber-Threat-Report_UK-Legal-Sector.pdf)
- "Many legal practices, especially smaller firms, chambers and individual practitioners, rely on an external IT services provider, making it challenging for them to assess for themselves whether the controls they have in place are appropriate to the risk they face. A small law firm with few resources could be devastated if caught up by (for example) a ransomware attack. They are more vulnerable to attack, perhaps via unpatched vulnerabilities on unmanaged devices, or due to untrained staff or poorly offboarded leavers. Once attacked, a relatively small financial or reputational loss may be disastrous." — [NCSC PDF](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/files/Cyber-Threat-Report_UK-Legal-Sector.pdf)
- "The SRA reported in 2020 that 75% of the solicitors' firms they visited for their cyber security thematic review had been the target of a cyber attack in the past"; "18 law firms were the victims of ransomware attacks in 2021"; "Nearly three-quarters of the UK's top-100 law firms have been affected by cyber attacks, and for smaller firms that have little or no dedicated cyber security and IT support, the risk of incidents like ransomware attacks is on the increase" — [NCSC PDF](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/files/Cyber-Threat-Report_UK-Legal-Sector.pdf)
- Of 40 firms the SRA visited, 30 had been targeted; the remaining 10 reported criminals had targeted their clients during transactions — [NCSC PDF](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/files/Cyber-Threat-Report_UK-Legal-Sector.pdf)
- The SRA published 278 scam alerts (people falsely claiming to be solicitors and firms) between January 2022 and January 2023 — [NCSC PDF](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/files/Cyber-Threat-Report_UK-Legal-Sector.pdf)
- Cyber Security Breaches Survey 2023: 32% of UK businesses identified cyber attacks; of the 48% that identified any breach or attack, phishing was the most common vector (79%) — [NCSC PDF](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/files/Cyber-Threat-Report_UK-Legal-Sector.pdf)
- BEC pattern: criminals "fabricate an email chain" and "send a doctored email with new bank details"; malicious emails are used to launch phishing against other law firms on the same matter — [NCSC PDF](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/files/Cyber-Threat-Report_UK-Legal-Sector.pdf)
- Remote working since March 2020 "created challenges for maintaining secure working practices and protecting client confidentiality"; home routers increased network exposure — [NCSC PDF](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/files/Cyber-Threat-Report_UK-Legal-Sector.pdf)
- Supply chain: "It is common, especially for smaller firms, to outsource the responsibilities for running, maintaining and securing their IT and data to specialist support companies. By far the greatest supply chain issue is a third party failing to adequately secure the systems that hold your sensitive data." — [NCSC PDF](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/files/Cyber-Threat-Report_UK-Legal-Sector.pdf)
- "The SRA has recently noted a decrease in the number of cyber security incidents being reported ... it is concerning that there may be a reluctance to report" — [NCSC PDF](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/files/Cyber-Threat-Report_UK-Legal-Sector.pdf)
- Top-100 firms spent an average of 0.46% of fee income on cyber security in 2022 (PwC; large-firm figure) — [NCSC PDF](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/files/Cyber-Threat-Report_UK-Legal-Sector.pdf)
- A City firm that lost client data in 2021 saw almost 8% wiped off its share value within an hour (listed firm) — [NCSC PDF](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/files/Cyber-Threat-Report_UK-Legal-Sector.pdf)

**UK: SRA cyber security thematic review (fieldwork 2016-2019; findings presented 30 October 2019; report September 2020)**
- 40 firms that had suffered attacks over three years; over £4m of client money stolen from 23 firms; insurers repaid most; 18 firms paid about £400,000 of their own money; 11 of 40 had inadequate policies and 10 inadequate controls; only 5 held Cyber Essentials Plus (all judged to have good controls) — [Legal Futures, 31 Oct 2019](https://www.legalfutures.co.uk/latest-news/review-spells-out-toll-of-cyber-crime-on-law-firms/print/); roughly £3.6m covered by insurance — [Today's Conveyancer](https://todaysconveyancer.co.uk/sra-order-cyber-crime-thematic-review-legal-sector/)

- Small-firm email modification fraud case: £400,000 transferred to fraudsters; insurer recovered all but a £5,000 excess; Legal Ombudsman awarded the client £900 — [Legal Futures](https://www.legalfutures.co.uk/latest-news/review-spells-out-toll-of-cyber-crime-on-law-firms/print/)

## 5. Everyday security pains small firms report

### Takeaway
The documented day-to-day problems are: client communication over WhatsApp on personal phones with no policy and no file capture; email as the main channel for bank details and documents; missing MFA and unpatched legacy systems; total reliance on an outsourced IT provider; home working; insurer and lender questions about controls and Cyber Essentials; and staff who say they do not understand cyber risk.

### Cited Findings
- WhatsApp: the SRA's asylum thematic review (31 July 2024, 25 firms) found WhatsApp had become the "preferred communications tool"; most firms had no "clear, written usage policy" on whether fee-earners should use WhatsApp on personal phones; "This poses data protection risks and the potential for exploitation of vulnerable clients"; most case management systems did not automatically save instant messages; 17 of 25 heads of asylum work (also COLPs) had not read the SRA's warning notices — [Legal Futures, 31 Jul 2024](https://www.legalfutures.co.uk/latest-news/law-firms-need-whatsapp-policies-when-advising-asylum-clients); [Law360](https://www.law360.com/articles/1863487)
- MacInnes v DWF Law LLP [2025] EWHC 3252 (SCCO): WhatsApp messages charged for form part of the solicitor's file and may have to be disclosed; the firm's failure to disclose them put it in breach of a peremptory order — [Legal Futures](https://www.legalfutures.co.uk/latest-news/whatsapp-messages-form-part-of-firms-file-if-they-charge-for-them); [Forbes Solicitors](https://www.forbessolicitors.co.uk/articles/whatsapp-messages-can-form-part-of-a-solicitors-file-the-defendant-firm-was-therefore-in-breach-of-a-peremptory-order)
- Consumer messaging risks listed by litigators: inadvertent disclosure, weak audit trails, record-keeping difficulty, and end-to-end encryption preventing firm monitoring — [Civil Litigation Brief](https://www.civillitigationbrief.com/category/avoiding-negligence-claims)
- Email: "No bank detail change communicated by email should ever be acted on without a separate, independently verified check" (Law Society guidance as summarised) — [Learnsignal](https://www.learnsignal.com/blog/residential-conveyancing-risk-and-fraud-guide/); the SDT said the solicitor who lost £290,000 "had plenty of time to get confirmation of the changed bank details by phone or in person" — [Law Society](https://www.lawsociety.org.uk/topics/regulation/solicitor-fined-for-failing-to-spot-friday-afternoon-cyber-fraud)
- MFA and passwords: Levales had no MFA on the compromised domain account — [ICO](https://ico.org.uk/action-weve-taken/enforcement/2024/10/levales-solicitors-llp/); Tuckers lacked MFA on key systems — [NCSC PDF](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/files/Cyber-Threat-Report_UK-Legal-Sector.pdf); DPP's attacker brute-forced an admin account on a legacy case management system — [teiss](https://www.teiss.co.uk/privacy/ico-fines-law-firm-dpp-60000-over-a-major-client-data-breach-15669); NCSC tells firms to check devices "to detect unchanged default passwords" and be "not solely reliant on passwords" — [NCSC PDF](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/files/Cyber-Threat-Report_UK-Legal-Sector.pdf)
- Outsourced IT: small firms cannot judge whether their provider's controls are adequate (NCSC, quoted in section 4); CTS outage left up to 200 firms unable to complete transactions — [Mortgage Solutions](https://www.mortgagesolutions.co.uk/news/2023/12/05/thousands-of-sales-in-limbo-after-conveyancing-cyberattack-%e2%80%92-reports/)
- Home working: NCSC on remote working from March 2020 and home routers — [NCSC PDF](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/files/Cyber-Threat-Report_UK-Legal-Sector.pdf)
- Staff knowledge: only two-thirds of staff at SRA-visited firms felt they understood IT and cyber issues; a fifth of firms had never run cyber training — [Today's Conveyancer](https://todaysconveyancer.co.uk/sra-order-cyber-crime-thematic-review-legal-sector/)
- Lost devices and paperwork (all sectors, ICO 2017/18): 438 paperwork loss/theft reports, 133 unencrypted device losses — [Legal IT Insider](https://legaltechnology.com/ico-legal-sector-data-breach-reports-surge-by-112-in-two-years/)
- Insurer questionnaires: "Some insurers now ask about the measures firms have taken to protect against scams, including security and IT systems and cyber accreditations"; demonstrating controls "might also help keep your PII premiums down" — [Law Society, cyber risk management page](https://www.lawsociety.org.uk/topics/small-firms/cyber-risk-management-and-the-sra-principles); US carriers likely require awareness training — [ABA 2022](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/law_practice/resources/tech-report/2022/cybersecurity/)
- Cyber Essentials: not mandated by the SRA or Law Society ("it's not a strict regulatory requirement for solicitors to purchase cyber insurance"; the Law Society president urged firms to consider Cyber Essentials) — [Law Society press release](https://lawsociety.org.uk/contact-or-visit-us/press-office/press-releases/seven-in-10-firms-dont-have-cyber-insurance); the SRA review recommended Cyber Essentials and DMARC — [Today's Conveyancer](https://todaysconveyancer.co.uk/sra-order-cyber-crime-thematic-review-legal-sector/); Cyber Essentials Plus assessment around £1,500 plus preparation (vendor) — [Forensic Control](https://forensiccontrol.com/sectors/law-firms/); corporate and public-sector clients increasingly require CE/CE+ as a condition of instruction (vendor claim) — [Forensic Control](https://forensiccontrol.com/sectors/law-firms/)
- Lender and panel requirements: the Conveyancing Association's Cyberfraud and Fraud Protocol asks firms to meet a "Cyber Safe Standard" that requires Cyber Essentials certification "or its equivalent", independently verified — [MPA Mag](https://www.mpamag.com/uk/mortgage-types/residential/conveyancing-association-unveils-cyberfraud-measures/373622); panel managers "can, for example, on the lenders behalf, ensure that the government's cyber essentials scheme is followed" — [Today's Conveyancer](https://todaysconveyancer.co.uk/?p=13218); "many lenders are asking conveyancing firms if they have specific Cyber Insurance protection in place" — [Conveyancing Association](https://www.conveyancingassociation.org.uk/cyber-insurance-are-you-covered/)
- Client security questionnaires (US): OCGs from financial services, healthcare and government clients require pre-approval, audit trails, role-based access and tool disclosure — [Intapp](https://www.intapp.com/blog/law-firm-ai-governance-ocg-compliance/); the Law Society, Bar Council and NCSC Industry 100 produced a risk-based cyber questionnaire for firms instructing chambers — [NCSC PDF](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/files/Cyber-Threat-Report_UK-Legal-Sector.pdf)
- Time lost to tools: each fee-earner loses 4.16 billable hours weekly to "ill-functioning technology" (Access Legal, vendor) — [Legal Futures blog](https://www.legalfutures.co.uk/blog/containing-the-spread-of-unapproved-ai)

### Inferences
- The WhatsApp problem is structural for consumer-facing small firms (asylum, family, crime): clients choose the channel, the firm has no capture, and the court now treats the messages as part of the file.
- Insurer and lender pressure is the practical driver of Cyber Essentials for small conveyancers, not regulation.

### Gaps
- No survey found on shared logins or shared passwords in law firms.
- No legal-sector-specific figure for lost USB sticks or devices after 2018.
- No published lender panel criteria (Lender Exchange, UK Finance Handbook) on cyber controls were found; only panel-manager and trade-body descriptions.
- No Law Society small-firm cyber survey for 2024-2025 retrieved.

## 6. Data-residency and on-premises requirements

### Takeaway
No UK or US regulator found mandates on-premises or in-country hosting for law firms. The pressure comes from UK GDPR international-transfer rules, client outside-counsel guidelines that ask "where data is stored", and some US judges' confidentiality certificates. Evidence of explicit residency demands is mostly from vendors and large-firm procurement.

### Cited Findings
- UK GDPR applies to barristers' and solicitors' AI inputs; the Bar Council warns of breach of "the General Data Protection Regulation (EU 2016/679) as retained in English law (UK GDPR)" — [Bar Council PDF, para 29](https://www.barcouncilethics.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/Considerations-when-using-ChatGPT-and-generative-artificial-intelligence-Nov-2025.pdf)
- JMW's reading of the SRA notice: firms should check "where it is processed" — [JMW](https://www.jmw.co.uk/blog/regulatory-and-disciplinary-proceedings/sra-ai-warning-notice-what-solicitors-and-law-firms-need-to-do-now)
- OCGs should specify "which client data can be used, which systems are approved, and where data is stored" — [Conventus Law](https://conventuslaw.com/report/how-to-write-outside-counsel-guidelines-that-govern-ai-assisted-work/)
- Large European firms ask "where client files are stored, where prompts are processed, which subprocessors touch the data, and whether anything enters model training" (vendor commentary, large-firm) — [Crimson Law](https://crimson.law/blog/security-compliance-enterprise-legal-ai); a British Legal IT Forum white paper (Feb 2026) lists UK/EU data-centre options with customer-controlled encryption as a baseline (vendor) — [Moterra white paper](https://www.britishlegalitforum.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/White-Paper-Moterra-for-legal-forum.pdf)
- A sovereign-cloud vendor argues reliance on US providers subject to the CLOUD Act "creates a direct conflict with EU and UK privacy principles" (vendor position) — [Impossible Cloud](https://www.impossiblecloud.com/magazine/legal-sector-data-residency)
- US federal: Judge Vaden's certificate that GAI use "didn't disclose proprietary information to unauthorized parties" — [Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/esg/ai-standing-orders-proliferate-as-federal-courts-forge-own-paths)
- Heppner: the court relied on the AI provider's privacy policy warning that prompts "may be shared further" — [WLF](https://www.wlf.org/2026/04/27/publishing/united-states-v-heppner-use-of-generative-ai-can-waive-privileges/)

### Inferences
- For a UK small firm the residency question is a GDPR transfer-mechanism question and a client-questionnaire question, not a regulator mandate; the firm's exposure is that it cannot answer "where is it processed" for a free consumer tool.

### Gaps
- No ICO statement on AI-tool international transfers retrieved.
- No evidence found of US state bars or courts requiring in-country hosting.
- The SCL article "AI Data Leaks & Shadow AI" (2025) could not be fetched (403).

## 7. What all this costs a small firm

### Takeaway
UK: only 28% of firms with up to 25 partners buy cyber insurance (2023), PII for conveyancing firms rose 25% to a £50,000 median, and firms doing conveyancing paid about £50,000 versus £7,000 for those that never did. Incident costs documented for small firms: £60,000 recovery plus £150,000 lost revenue and 14 days closed (ransomware); £400,000 diverted (email fraud); £60,000 ICO fine (DPP). Ransom demands in the legal sector averaged US$611,000 in 2025.

### Cited Findings
- Law Society PII research (July 2023; 600 firms with up to 25 partners): only 28% bought cyber insurance even though the SRA's 2021 minimum terms exclude first-party cyber losses; conveyancing firms' median premium rose 25% from £40,000 to £50,000; median across all firms rose over 12% — [Legal Futures](https://www.legalfutures.co.uk/latest-news/pii-premiums-rises-to-be-lower-than-last-year-says-law-society-research/print/); [Law Gazette](https://www.lawgazette.co.uk/news/pii-trends-seven-in-10-firms-still-lack-cyber-insurance/5116724.article); [Today's Conveyancer](https://todaysconveyancer.co.uk/?p=64123)
- Later Law Society PII survey: firms that did conveyancing in 2022/23 paid an average premium of £50,000 versus about £7,000 for firms that never did — [Today's Conveyancer](https://todaysconveyancer.co.uk/pii-costs-not-expected-fall-near-term/)
- A different Law Society figure: 44% of SRA firms purchased separate cyber cover at an average cost of 0.1% of turnover (different survey/year; does not reconcile with 28%) — [Law Society small firms page](https://www.lawsociety.org.uk/topics/small-firms/cyber-risk-management-and-the-sra-principles)
- Incident costs: £60,000 recovery and £150,000 lost revenue, 14 days closed (SRA case); £400,000 diverted with £5,000 excess; £400,000 total paid by 18 firms out of their own pockets — [Legal Futures](https://www.legalfutures.co.uk/latest-news/review-spells-out-toll-of-cyber-crime-on-law-firms/print/)
- Simplify Group outage reported at £6.8m (large conveyancer) — [NCSC PDF](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/files/Cyber-Threat-Report_UK-Legal-Sector.pdf)
- DPP Law fine £60,000 — [Enforcement Tracker](https://www.enforcementtracker.com/ETid-2566)
- Ransom demands: US$383,000 (2024) to US$611,000 (2025) average in the legal sector — [Insurance Business / QBE](https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/au/news/cyber/ransomware-groups-are-coming-for-law-firms-580610.aspx)
- Breach cost benchmarks (not small-firm specific): IBM professional-services average US$5.9m — [Sikich](https://www.sikich.com/insight/the-lawyers-guide-to-cybersecurity-in-2026/); a "US$5.08m legal sector" figure circulates in vendor material without a traceable primary source — [Huntress](https://www.huntress.com/industries/lawfirms/data-breach)
- Top-100 UK firms spend 0.46% of fee income on cyber (large-firm) — [NCSC PDF](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/files/Cyber-Threat-Report_UK-Legal-Sector.pdf)
- Cyber Essentials Plus assessment about £1,500 plus preparation (vendor) — [Forensic Control](https://forensiccontrol.com/sectors/law-firms/)
- Sanctions for hallucinations: Mata $5,000 — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mata_v._Avianca,_Inc.); 2025 US distribution: 37 cases at $1,000-$5,000, 4 over $50,000 — [MSBA](https://www.msba.org/site/content/News-and-Publications/News/General-News/Confronting_the_Hard%20Numbers_Behind_Lawyers_AI_and_Phantom_Precedents.aspx); wasted costs orders in Ayinde — [Judgment](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewhc/admin/2025/1383)
- Time: 4.16 billable hours per fee-earner per week lost to poor technology (vendor estimate) — [Legal Futures blog](https://www.legalfutures.co.uk/blog/containing-the-spread-of-unapproved-ai); 14 days closed in the SRA ransomware case — [Legal Futures](https://www.legalfutures.co.uk/latest-news/review-spells-out-toll-of-cyber-crime-on-law-firms/print/)

### Inferences
- For a conveyancing small firm, PII alone is a £50,000-a-year line item driven partly by fraud exposure; most still do not buy the separate cyber cover that would pay for their own losses after the 2021 SRA minimum-terms change.
- The documented small-firm incident bill (roughly £200,000 for a two-week ransomware closure) is of the same order as a year's PII premium, which explains why insurers and lenders now ask about controls.
