## 1. Corporate AI training and workshops: market size, prices, buyers, Saudi and UAE programmes, licensing

### Takeaway
The training market in the Gulf is large and growing, but the state gives away most basic AI training for free and a licence (TVTC in Saudi Arabia, KHDA in Dubai) is needed to sell training as a training provider. For a foreign individual who is employed on a Saudi work permit, selling training in his own name is not legally open; it has to go through a licensed company, a licensed partner, or his employer.

Licensing and legal access
- TVTC (primary): foreign investors may obtain a training licence in Saudi Arabia; the licence lasts five years; it needs a commercial registration, municipal licence, Civil Defence certificate, accredited programmes and staff; training without a TVTC licence is a violation; "e-training only" licences are not issued (physical premises required, remote delivery needs extra approval and a National eLearning Center licence); individual trainers cannot be licensed on their own, only facilities and their programmes — [TVTC FAQs](https://tvtc.gov.sa/en/ContactUS/FAQs/Pages/default.aspx)
- Setup path for a foreign-owned training company: MISA investment licence (3 to 10 business days; vendor says MISA fees are suspended in 2026), commercial registration, Chamber membership (about SAR 2,000 to 3,000 a year), TVTC licence with premises inspection and per-course accreditation (30 to 90 days), then Qiwa, GOSI, ZATCA and Saudization (Nitaqat) duties. The same guide says in-house-only corporate training "usually does not require a public licence, but confirm with TVTC". Setup packages from SAR 36,999 (vendor) — [Noble Core KSA](https://noblecoreksa.com/training-center-saudi-arabia/)
- Without MISA approval a Saudi partner must hold at least 25%; Nitaqat quotas apply above five employees — [Legal 500 / Knightsbridge Group](https://www.legal500.com/firms/244096-knightsbridge-group/c-united-arab-emirates/news-and-developments/how-to-set-up-an-education-training-services-company-in-saudi-arabia)
- Saudi Labor Law text (primary; article numerals did not render in the extracted copy, this is the clause commonly cited as Article 39): "an employer may not allow his worker to work for others, and a worker may not work for other employers" and "An employer may not allow a worker to work for his own account and a worker may not work for his own account." — [HRSD copy of the Labor Law](https://www.hrsd.gov.sa/sites/default/files/2017-05/LABOR%20LAW.pdf)
- Dubai: a trade licence alone is not enough; training institutes need a KHDA Educational Services Permit, valid one year. Fees seen: AED 15,000 for 1 to 2 activities, AED 18,000 for 3 to 4, AED 20,000 for 5 to 6, AED 25,000 for more than seven; processing 8 working days (snippet; the KHDA page refused the connection) — [KHDA service page](https://web.khda.gov.ae/en/Services/Training-Institute-Permit-Services/Issuing-an-Educational-Services-Permit-for-a-Train); [Business Dubai guide](https://www.businessdubai.ae/blogs/training-institute-business-setup-dubai)

### Inferences
- Basic "what is AI" training for Saudi nationals is not a market a private newcomer can charge for: over 1.1 million people got it free in 2025 and the public sector round (SAMAI 2) is state-run. Paid demand sits above that level: executive briefings, role-specific workshops (bank, hospital, legal), and adoption programmes tied to a real rollout.
- Price ladder that the sources support: open-enrolment seat USD 450 (one day) to about USD 6,000 (five days); a private half-day executive session about USD 4,500 (GBP 3,500) at boutique level; an adoption programme USD 55,000 and up. My calculation: a 5-day Gulf public course at USD 5,950 with 10 to 15 seats grosses about USD 60,000 to 90,000 for the provider, before venue, sales and trainer cost. A solo trainer hired by such a provider gets the day rate (hundreds to low thousands of dollars), not the seat revenue. The money is in owning the provider or the client relationship, not in being the trainer.
- Buyers shown by the data are large enterprises (65% of GCC spend), banks and insurers (35%), and government entities. This matches the brief's guess (banks, government) but nothing found confirms family groups or universities as buyers.
- Legal reading for this person: employed on a work permit, he may not train for other employers or on his own account. The lawful routes are (a) training sold and invoiced by his employer, (b) his own MISA-licensed company with a TVTC licence, or (c) delivering as a named expert under a licensed Saudi training provider's contract, with his employer's consent. "In-house consulting workshops" sold by a properly registered services company are the grey area the vendor guide flags; it needs a direct answer from TVTC. This is a reading of the sources, not legal advice.
- HRDF subsidy only helps when the training provider is HRDF-registered and the trainees are Saudi nationals, so it favours licensed local institutes; a small foreign firm would need a local licensed partner to benefit.

### Gaps
- No verified in-house day rate for AI executive training in Riyadh or Dubai. All firm numbers are UK, Europe or public-seat prices.
- Nothing found on SDAIA Academy, KAUST Academy, MCIT skills programmes or Misk beyond the Tuwaiq link (search allowance ran out).
- Not confirmed whether SAMAI 2 or the UAE 80,000-employee programme pay private providers, and how providers are chosen.
- HRDF caps and the KHDA fee table come from search summaries, not opened primary pages.
- The Labor Law copy is the 2005 text; amendments that took effect in 2025 were not checked for changes to this clause. Premium Residency and freelance-permit options for non-Saudis were not researched.
- GCC market-size figures conflict by a factor of about nine between report sellers (USD 1.4 billion versus USD 12.9 billion).

## 2. Paid speaking: fees by tier, how Gulf events pick speakers, sponsors paying to speak, bureaus

### Takeaway
Paid speaking is a small business for almost everyone in it: the typical full-time "thought leader" earns about USD 153,000 a year in total revenue after roughly 12 to 15 years, and unknown practitioners at conferences usually speak for free. Fees above USD 25,000 go to people with a famous employer, a book or a public office behind them.

### Cited Findings
- AI keynote fee bands in 2026 from an international bureau: USD 5,000 to 10,000 for emerging voices and regional specialists; USD 10,000 to 25,000 is "the band where most corporate keynote work happens" (people with books, ex-Big Tech roles, recognised academic posts); USD 25,000 to 50,000 for recognised names with global track record; USD 50,000-plus for former Google or OpenAI executives, government leaders and best-selling authors (vendor) — [Speakers Associates AI guide 2026](https://www.speakersassociates.com/go/ai-guide-2026/)
- 2024 benchmark survey of 340 event organisers and 378 experienced speakers (older, survey): average keynote budget USD 22,449; just under half of organisers budget under USD 10,000; 47% budget USD 10,000 to 50,000; only 9% budget over USD 50,000. Speakers' average reported fee about USD 14,000; only 3% charge over USD 30,000. About 70% of planners involve two to five colleagues in the choice — [PCMA](https://pcma.org/survey-explores-speaker-planner-dynamics)
- SpeakerFlow 2026 State of the Industry (survey; 216 thought leaders, 13 countries, 2025 results): median total business revenue USD 153,000; average USD 313,000; top 10% average USD 1.37 million. Bands: under USD 50,000 = 20.6%; 50,000 to 100,000 = 13.7%; 100,000 to 250,000 = 30.9%; 250,000 to 500,000 = 18.1%; 500,000 to 750,000 = 7.8%; 750,000 to 1 million = 3.9%; over 1 million = 4.9%. Average time in business 15 years, median 12. Only 36% grew revenue in 2025 (60% in 2024); average revenue is about 16% below peak — [SpeakerFlow](https://pages.speakerflow.com/2026-ssi-report/)
- Same survey, where the money comes from: referrals and word of mouth produce 64.7% of revenue; direct outreach 12.6%; inbound content and SEO 6.5%; bureaus 6.5%; social media 2.3% of revenue while taking 31.5% of marketing time. Speakers whose main channel is social media average USD 69,000; referral-led average USD 280,000. Those with bureau relationships average USD 430,000 versus USD 238,000 without; 61% do not work with bureaus. Speakers who gave four or more free talks in 2025 earned USD 80,000 less than those who gave none. Those who license their content as a separate revenue stream average USD 633,000 versus USD 243,000 for those who do not — [SpeakerFlow](https://pages.speakerflow.com/2026-ssi-report/)
- Beginner tier: USD 500 to 2,500 per talk in years 0 to 2, often USD 0 to 1,000; "many new event speakers give free speeches"; association and non-profit events typically pay in exposure; regional conferences USD 1,000 to 5,000; virtual talks 50 to 75% below in-person (guide with no cited research) — [Talks.co](https://talks.co/p/speaking-fees/)
- A large US bureau booked USD 48 million of talks in 2025, 88% in person, with AI the leading topic — [BigSpeak](https://www.bigspeak.com/2025-in-review-what-our-data-tells-us-about-the-future-of-speaking/)

### Inferences
- Three working tiers follow from the sources: unknown practitioner = free to about USD 2,500 (and at trade shows usually unpaid or sponsor-funded); known founder or author = USD 5,000 to 25,000; celebrity or ex-Big Tech = USD 25,000 to 85,000-plus. The person in the brief is in the first tier today.
- My calculation: even at a strong USD 10,000 fee, 30 paid talks a year is USD 300,000 and requires bureau and referral demand that takes most speakers a decade to build. This is a good living but not "very big money", and it is still selling days.
- The SpeakerFlow split (social media 2.3% of revenue, referrals 64.7%) says speaking income is a relationship business; an audience on LinkedIn or YouTube does not by itself produce speaking fees.
- At the big Gulf shows the stage is filled by invited senior names and, on the evidence of the sponsorship tiers, probably by sponsors too (not confirmed). For a small agency the realistic entries are: apply through the open LEAP form, speak on a side stage or partner track, or buy a slot as part of a stand. The stage is marketing spend, not income.

- No data on fees paid to Urdu or Arabic-language AI speakers.

## 3. Paid courses, cohorts and communities; Pakistan and Urdu/Hindi market; Arabic market

### Takeaway
Course and community income is very top-heavy, the typical result is around USD 1,000 a month, and in Pakistan the price level is close to free because the biggest AI programmes charge nothing. The largest South Asian creator course business on record closed in May 2026 with its founder saying AI's role in the decision was "huge".

### Cited Findings
- Maven (cohort platform) says instructors earn about USD 20,000 per cohort on average and keep 90% of revenue less payment fees; the page also states over 15,000 students and USD 14 million earned by instructors "to date" (vendor marketing; undated figure) — [Maven course accelerator](https://maven.com/maven/course-accelerator)
- Skool paid communities, tracked 2 September 2026: 48,100 communities; 32.6% charge a fee (about 15,700); median modelled revenue USD 1,200 a month; 52.2% of paid communities exceed USD 1,000 a month; about 2,500 exceed USD 10,000 a month; median price USD 29 a month. Method: price times current members, "a ceiling, not a bank statement" (modelled estimate) — [Profitable.app](https://profitable.app/skool/stats)
- Udemy cut the instructor share of subscription revenue from 25% in 2023 to 20% in January 2024, on a schedule down to 15% by 2026 (primary, older filing) — [Udemy SEC exhibit](https://sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1607939/000160793923000181/exhibit991-xinstructorle.htm)
- "85,000-plus Udemy instructors earned USD 168 million in 2025" and the instructors' share of Udemy revenue fell from 40% in 2019 to an estimated 24% in 2024 (snippet; statistics aggregators, original not opened) — [GrabOn Udemy statistics](https://www.grabon.in/indulge/tech/udemy-statistics/); [EzyCourse](https://ezycourse.com/blog/why-udemy-creators-are-frustrated)
- Ankur Warikoo (India) announced on 16 May 2026 that he is shutting his online courses business after five years: over INR 100 crore revenue, INR 25 crore profit, about 500,000 students since 2020. Asked whether AI drove the decision he answered "huge" (self-reported figures, widely reported) — [Channel iAM](https://en.channeliam.com/2026/05/16/ankur-warikoo-closes-online-courses-business/)

### Inferences
- My calculation on Warikoo: INR 100 crore over five years from 500,000 students is about INR 2,000 (about USD 24) per student and about USD 12 million in total at roughly 85 rupees to the dollar. It needed an audience in the millions, and it was closed by its owner. This is the best available evidence that low-priced, mass "learn AI" courses are a shrinking business in 2026.
- My calculation on Udemy, if the snippet figures are right: USD 168 million across 85,000 instructors averages under USD 2,000 a year each.
- Pakistan price anchors: free (PIAIC, iCodeGuru) up to PKR 31,000 (about USD 110) for a 40-day paid cohort. A Pakistan-facing course cannot produce large income without six-figure audiences; its real value to an agency owner is recruiting and brand, which is how Xeven appears to use Hope to Skills.
- Median Skool community (about USD 1,200 a month at most, before churn) and a Maven cohort (about USD 20,000, vendor figure) confirm that for someone with 8,400 followers a paid course is side income.
- The better-paying versions of "teaching" are the ones in section 1: company-paid seats at USD 450 to 6,000 and custom programmes, not consumer courses.

## 4. Content as a sales engine: founder-led LinkedIn and YouTube and B2B pipeline

### Takeaway
The evidence that good expert content helps win B2B deals is strong, but it works slowly and through being already known when the buyer starts looking, not through viral reach. Hard numbers on meetings and revenue per post are almost all self-reported.

### Cited Findings
- Edelman-LinkedIn 2025 B2B Thought Leadership Impact Report (seventh annual; survey; read through secondary reports): more than 40% of B2B deals stall because of internal misalignment; among "hidden buyers" (finance, legal, operations people who do not meet sales), 64% spend over an hour a week on thought leadership, 55% use it to assess vendors, 95% say strong thought leadership makes them more open to outreach, 86% want content that challenges their assumptions, 73% use it to judge the thinking a company would bring, 79% are more likely to back a vendor that publishes high-quality thought leadership — [Fifth Quadrant summary](https://www.fifthquadrant.com.au/thought-leadership-research-wins-deals)
- Same report: 53% say that when a company's thought leadership is strong, brand recognition matters less (snippet) — [Demand Gen Report](https://www.demandgenreport.com/industry-news/news-brief/marketers-need-to-gear-content-to-hidden-b2b-buyers-edelman-linkedin/49901/)
- 6sense Buyer Experience Report 2025 (via a statistics round-up): 95% of B2B purchases go to a vendor already on the buyer's day-one shortlist; 92% of buyers start with at least one vendor in mind; 94% use large language models during buying — [Oktopost](https://www.oktopost.com/blog/b2b-social-media-statistics/)
- LinkedIn B2B Institute with the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute: only about 5% of B2B buyers are in the market at any time; 95% are not (older, 2021) — [Marketing Week](https://www.marketingweek.com/peter-weinberg-jon-lombardo-95-5-rule)
- Metricool LinkedIn study 2026 (673,658 posts): personal profiles average 2.60% engagement, company pages 1.74% (63% higher for people) — [Oktopost](https://www.oktopost.com/blog/b2b-social-media-statistics/)
- RevBoss founder-led marketing benchmarks 2026 (agency's own client panel: 33 profiles at 28 B2B companies of USD 1 million to 50 million revenue, 1 January to 25 August 2026, 1,202 posts, 54,710 connection invites) — [RevBoss](https://www.revboss.com/founder-led-marketing-benchmarks):
  - Median cadence 1.7 posts a week; accounts above the median gained about twice the followers (737 versus 373 per six months).
  - Median post 502 impressions; 90th percentile 3,334; 25% of posts pass 1,000; 4.7% pass 10,000; the top 5% of posts produce 66% of all impressions.
  - Founder accounts get 2.1 times the impressions of company pages (520 versus 244 median).
  - The median post draws 9 identifiable professionals; the median account surfaces 553 identifiable engagers in six months.
  - Connection invites to target buyers are accepted 15.0% of the time (best quarter of campaigns 22.6% or more, best when sent to people who already engaged); follow-up messages get 21.4% replies (809 of 3,774).
  - Only 28% of the median founder's existing connections fit the ideal customer profile (range 6% to 51%); about half of audience growth came from targeted invites, not from content reach.
  - The report does not measure meetings or revenue.
- Claim that inbound leads from founder content close at 14.6% versus 1.7% for cold outbound (low quality; appears on a consultant's blog without a study behind it) — [Koka Sexton](https://kokasexton.com/founder-led-growth-on-linkedin/)
- Unify (software company) says founder-led LinkedIn content drove USD 15 million in pipeline (self-reported; page could not be opened, title only) — [Unify](https://www.unifygtm.com/explore/founder-led-content-how-we-drove-15m-in-pipeline-via-linkedin)
- For professional speakers, social media takes 31.5% of marketing time and yields 2.3% of revenue; referrals yield 64.7% (survey) — [SpeakerFlow](https://pages.speakerflow.com/2026-ssi-report/)
- Fractional executives: 92.8% get clients through network referrals; cold outreach 19.2%; paid ads 13.2% (survey, 2024) — [Fractionus summary of Frak report](https://fractionus.com/blog/fractional-work-statistics-2025-income-market-data)
- YouTube case: Liam Ottley's channel grew alongside his agency Morningside AI (founded 2022), first as marketing for the agency, then as an education business for people who want to start AI agencies; by the end of 2024 the agency "was absorbed into a broader content brand" (directory profile; its subscriber counts are internally inconsistent) — [Automation Atlas](https://automationatlas.io/creators/liam-ottley/)
- A third-party teardown estimates his funnel at about 130 booked calls a month, 30% close rate, about 40 buyers a month and USD 80,000 to 200,000 a month from organic traffic, selling a programme priced around USD 5,000 to 7,000 (unverified estimate from a search summary, not his accounts) — [Substack teardown](https://sabahudinmurtic.substack.com/p/330000-monthly-funnel-by-liam-outtley)

### Inferences
- The mechanism the data supports is: most buyers are not buying today (95-5), they shortlist before talking to anyone (6sense), and people they never meet in sales calls read expert content and can block or back a vendor (Edelman). So content pays by putting the founder on the shortlist months later. It should be judged on named target accounts engaging, not on follower counts.
- Benchmark for this person: the brief gives about 8,400 followers at about 2.4% reach, which is about 200 views a post. The RevBoss median is 502 impressions a post, and even there the median post reaches only 9 identifiable professionals. So content alone will not produce inbound at his current size; the working pattern in the RevBoss data is content plus targeted connection invites and follow-up messages to people who engaged (15% accept, 21% reply).
- Audience make-up matters more than size: the median founder's network was only 28% target buyers. A Pakistani engineer's network is likely to be peers and job seekers, not Gulf executives, so the first job is rebuilding who follows him, which the RevBoss data says comes mostly from targeted invites.
- The Liam Ottley case shows where YouTube content about "how to build AI things" naturally converts: to learners and would-be agency owners, not to enterprise buyers. Content aimed at peers builds a course business; content aimed at a buyer industry builds a services pipeline. He must choose the audience on purpose.
- Formats: nothing found gives a controlled comparison of case studies versus teardowns versus webinars. The Edelman finding that buyers want content that "challenges assumptions" and shows "the thinking a company would bring" points to specific teardowns and case studies over general AI news.
- The 14.6% versus 1.7% close-rate figure matches a much older, widely recycled statistic about search leads versus outbound leads; it should not be used.

### Gaps
- No independent study gives cadence, audience size or months needed before inbound deals appear. RevBoss shows cadence and reach but stops before meetings and revenue.
- Refine Labs and Gong content-attribution data were not retrieved (search allowance ran out).
- The Edelman report itself would not open; sample size and survey dates are not captured, and one secondary source gives 63% where another gives 64% for the same item.
- No YouTube-specific B2B conversion data found.

## 6. Advisory and fractional roles: pay, who hires, equity, realism for five years' experience and no finished degree

### Takeaway
Fractional executives typically earn USD 5,000 to 8,000 a month per client and hold two or three clients, and almost all of them are 15-plus-year veterans who get work through referrals. For someone with about five years' experience it is a weak main route today; advisor equity is small (about 0.25% to 1%) and only pays if the company exits.

### Cited Findings

### Inferences
- My calculation: three clients at USD 5,000 to 8,000 a month is USD 180,000 to 290,000 a year. That is the realistic ceiling of a solo fractional practice and it is still time for money.

With about five years and no finished degree, he would be selling against 15-year veterans to boards that screen on pedigree. A more reachable version is an "AI advisor" retainer for small and mid-sized owners (clinics, family firms) who buy on trust and results, priced at the advisory band (USD 4,000 to 10,000 a month at US rates; no Gulf figure found), and best attached to delivery work by his agency.

Revenue share or equity in something he co-owns (a product or joint venture built by his team for an industry partner) is the only version of "advisory" in these sources that could reach very big money. No source found describes a standard revenue-share advisory deal.

## 7. Skill polish: evidence-backed ways to improve executive-level selling and presenting quickly

### Takeaway
The best-supported fast improvement is reviewing recordings of his own sales calls against a simple discovery structure, with regular coaching; the named frameworks (SPIN, MEDDICC, Challenger) all have supporting numbers, but most of those numbers come from the firms that sell them.

### Cited Findings
- Gong analysis of 326,000 sales calls (vendor's own dataset, article updated March 2025 and March 2026): closed-won deals averaged 57% seller talk time to 43% listening; top performers keep the same ratio whether they win or lose, while low performers swing by 10 percentage points; won deals have shorter seller monologues — [Gong](https://www.gong.io/blog/talk-to-listen-conversion-ratio/); contradicted in direction by secondary write-ups that quote Gong as "43% talking to 57% listening" for top calls — [Coffee.ai](https://coffee.ai/articles/analyze-sales-calls-in-gong)
- Gong 2026 study of 519,000 discovery calls, as quoted second-hand: sellers asking 7 to 10 questions closed at 66% versus 46% for 1 to 6 questions (snippet, not verified at source) — [Pulse RevOps](https://pulserevops.com/knowledge/q12718)
- Customer case: Mintel raised win rates 34% after building a call-review and coaching habit with Gong (vendor case study) — [Gong](https://www.gong.io/case-studies/research-recommendations-and-reality-how-gong-helped-mintel-increase-win-rates-by-34/)
- Regular call analysis is claimed to lift close rates 20 to 30% "when executed consistently", and gains fade when reviews stop (vendor blog) — [Coffee.ai](https://coffee.ai/articles/analyze-sales-calls-in-gong)
- SPIN: Huthwaite data is quoted as a 17% close-rate improvement within two quarters when SPIN is combined with manager-led coaching (snippet; vendor-origin) — [Pulse RevOps](https://pulserevops.com/knowledge/q12718)
- Challenger: based on a CEB (now Gartner) study of more than 6,000 sales reps; 40% of top performers fit the Challenger profile, rising to 54% in complex sales (older, 2011 research, quoted second-hand) — [Bliro](https://www.bliro.io/en/blog/sales-frameworks-in-b2b-meddic-bant-spin-co---which-framework-suits-your-team)
- MEDDPICC: organisations that fully adopt it report 18% higher win rates and 24% larger deals; teams with 90%-plus field coverage see 61% win rates (vendor blog, no method shown) — [Coffee.ai](https://coffee.ai/articles/meddpicc-success-rate-benchmarks)
- Korn Ferry 2026 research is quoted as showing 27% higher win rates where teams combine methods rather than use one (snippet) — [Pulse RevOps](https://pulserevops.com/knowledge/q12718)
- For speaker-consultants: those who track pipeline in a CRM average USD 401,000 revenue versus USD 155,000 for those who track "in their head"; 65% of lost deals are lost after the first conversation (survey) — [SpeakerFlow](https://pages.speakerflow.com/2026-ssi-report/)

### Inferences
- What the evidence agrees on, across vendors: ask more and better discovery questions, avoid long monologues, be consistent, and review recordings regularly with someone who coaches. Which branded framework is used matters less than using one every time.
- A practical fast programme that fits the sources: record every sales and discovery call; review two a week against a short checklist (number of questions, longest monologue, whether money, decision process and next step were covered, which is the core of MEDDICC); use SPIN-style question order for discovery; use a Challenger-style "here is what clinics like yours get wrong" opening for executive talks.
- Since he is already strong at speaking, the gap is more likely discovery and follow-up discipline than stage skill. The SpeakerFlow finding that most deals are lost right after first contact points the same way.
- The second-hand Gong, Huthwaite and Korn Ferry figures came from search summaries of vendor blogs; which of the listed pages carries each figure was not confirmed, so quote them only as "reported".

### Gaps
- No independent, controlled evidence found for any framework; almost every number is vendor-sourced or second-hand.
- No research found on Toastmasters outcomes or on paid sales coaching outcomes (search allowance ran out before this was covered).
- The Gong talk-ratio figure is quoted in opposite directions by different pages; treat the exact ratio as unsettled.

### Takeaway
The cases found show content building large businesses in two ways: as free education that feeds a services company with talent and reputation (the Pakistani pattern), or as a course business sold to learners (the Indian and YouTube pattern, now under pressure from AI). ### Inferences
- What made content convert in these cases: (1) one clear audience and language (Urdu learners for Malik and Khan; Hindi-English learners for Naik and Warikoo; aspiring agency owners for Ottley); (2) steady teaching over years, not occasional talks; (3) a business behind the content that could absorb the demand (a services firm, a paid cohort, a platform); (4) scale in the hundreds of thousands of followers before the content business itself was large.
- The Xeven pattern is the closest match to the person in the brief: a Pakistani AI services company with Gulf offices whose founder teaches in public. There the teaching feeds hiring, credibility and deal flow for the services and product company; the big money, if any, is in the company, not in fees for teaching. No audited revenue was found, so its size is not proven.
- Overall answer to the main question, drawing on all eight sections: as a main business, speaking, training and advisory for a person at his current profile top out around USD 150,000 to 300,000 a year (median thought-leader revenue USD 153,000 after 12-plus years; fractional practices USD 180,000 to 290,000 by my calculation), need a decade of referrals, and in Saudi Arabia cannot lawfully be done in his own name while employed. As a sales engine for a company he owns, the evidence is much stronger: buyers shortlist vendors they already know, hidden decision-makers read expert content, founder profiles outperform company pages, and industry teaching sessions put him in front of owners. "Very big money, not salary or per-project fees" is only reachable in these sources through ownership (equity in a product or services company, or a sale like iNeuron's), with public expertise as the way customers and partners find it.
- The two risks most visible in the evidence: teaching peers instead of buyers (which builds a course audience, and course income is collapsing at the low end), and spending most marketing time on social posting, which produced 2.3% of revenue for professional speakers.

### Gaps
- No Middle East case of a technical founder using content or speaking to build a large services or product firm was found; this is a real hole, not proof that none exists.
- No verified revenue for Xeven Solutions, Panacloud/PIAIC or Morningside AI; all figures are self-reported counts of students or clients.
- Varun Mayya, Harkirat Singh (100xDevs) and other Indian technical creators were searched but no reliable revenue figures surfaced.
- The Unify case could not be read beyond its headline claim.
